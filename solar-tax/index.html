Why Material Participation Matters for Solar

Solar installations generate substantial tax deductions — accelerated depreciation under IRC §168, bonus depreciation elections, and the Investment Tax Credit (ITC) under IRC §48. But there's a catch: the IRS classifies these as passive activity losses by default under IRC §469.

Passive losses can only offset passive income. If you have no passive income, your solar deductions sit unused — carrying forward indefinitely with no immediate benefit.

IRC §469 — Passive Activity Loss Rules
A taxpayer's deduction for passive activity losses is limited to passive activity income. Losses exceeding passive income are suspended and carry forward until the activity generates income or is fully disposed of.

The solution is material participation. If you materially participate in the solar activity, those losses become non-passive — deductible directly against your W-2 wages, business income, or any other ordinary income. For a taxpayer in the 37% bracket, a $500,000 depreciation deduction becomes a $185,000 tax refund.

The Seven IRS Material Participation Tests

Under Temp. Reg. §1.469-5T, the IRS provides seven tests. You only need to satisfy one in a given tax year. We structure our clients around the most achievable test for their situation.

✦ Your Primary Test
101 hrs

Test 3: More than 100 hours, and not less than any other individual (including employees and contractors).

Alternative
500 hrs

Test 1: More than 500 hours of participation. Most common for sole operators with full control.

Alternative
Substantially All

Test 2: Substantially all participation in the activity is by you and your spouse.

History-Based
5 of 10 yrs

Test 5: Materially participated in 5 of the prior 10 taxable years — useful for established investors.

⚠️ The "Not Less Than Anyone Else" Requirement — Test 3
Your hours must equal or exceed the hours of every other participant — including contractors, property managers, and on-site personnel. You need contemporaneous records of your hours and a clear understanding of how much time contractors are logging.

What Activities Count

Not all time counts. The IRS looks for bona fide participation in operations, management, or oversight — not passive investor review.

IRS Documentation Standards

This is where most solar tax strategies fail an audit. The IRS requires contemporaneous records — logs kept at or near the time the activity occurred, not reconstructed at year-end.

Temp. Reg. §1.469-5T(f)(4) — Proof of Participation
The extent of an individual's participation may be established by any reasonable means. Contemporaneous daily time reports or logs are not required if the extent of such participation may be established by other reasonable means. However, post-event ballpark guesstimates are not sufficient.

What Good Records Look Like

  • Date of each activity
  • Specific description — what was actually done
  • Time spent — start/end or total hours
  • Supporting evidence — emails, invoices, site photos, meeting notes
✅ Best Practice: Log each activity within 24–48 hours of occurrence. Courts consistently uphold real-time logs and reject year-end reconstructions. Use our hour tracker below — it's built specifically for this purpose and generates a printable audit report.

What to Avoid

  • Vague entries like "worked on solar project — 4 hours"
  • Round-number estimates or weekly approximations
  • Year-end summaries created before filing
  • No documentation — courts have denied deductions entirely in this scenario

How We Structure Solar Investments

Getting material participation right requires more than logging hours. The investment structure, entity type, and roles of any property managers all affect whether losses are ultimately non-passive.

Step 1 — Identify the right test

We assess your situation: do you have contractors or employees involved? How much time can you realistically commit? The 101-hour test (Test 3) is often the most achievable for investors with active management roles.

Step 2 — Structure contractor roles carefully

For Test 3, contractors' hours count against you. We define contractor scopes narrowly — ensuring property managers handle only administrative tasks while you retain decision-making oversight.

Step 3 — Set up a contemporaneous logging system

We provide clients with our material participation tracker — a secure, login-gated portal where you log activities in real time, monitor your progress to the 101-hour threshold, and generate audit-ready PDF reports.

Step 4 — Coordinate with bonus depreciation timing

IRC §168(k) bonus depreciation for solar equipment has a phase-down schedule. We coordinate the depreciation election with your material participation year to maximize the deduction where it creates the most tax benefit.

What Happens in an Audit

IRS scrutiny of passive activity claims is increasing — solar tax strategies are specifically flagged as an area of focus. Here's what to expect:

  • The IRS requests your contemporaneous activity logs for the year
  • Agents compare your claimed hours against contractor 1099s and payroll records
  • They may interview contractors to verify scope of involvement
  • Without adequate documentation, deductions are disallowed and 20–40% penalties may apply
⚠️ Accuracy penalty risk: If the IRS disallows a material participation claim for lack of documentation, the taxpayer typically owes a 20% accuracy-related penalty under IRC §6662 on top of back taxes and interest. For a $500,000 deduction at 37%, that's $185,000 in taxes + $37,000 in penalties.
