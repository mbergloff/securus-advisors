Why Material Participation Matters for Solar

Solar installations generate substantial tax deductions — accelerated depreciation under IRC §168, bonus depreciation elections, and the Investment Tax Credit (ITC) under IRC §48. But there's a catch: the IRS classifies these as passive activity losses by default under IRC §469.

Passive losses can only offset passive income. If you have no passive income, your solar deductions sit unused, carrying forward year after year with no immediate benefit.

IRC §469 — Passive Activity Loss Rules
A taxpayer's deduction for passive activity losses is limited to passive activity income. Losses exceeding passive income are suspended and carry forward until the activity generates income or is fully disposed of.

The solution is material participation. If you materially participate in the solar activity, losses become non-passive — deductible directly against your W-2 wages, business income, or any other ordinary income. For a taxpayer in the 37% bracket, this transforms a $500,000 depreciation deduction into a $185,000 tax refund.

The Seven IRS Material Participation Tests

Under Temp. Reg. §1.469-5T, the IRS provides seven tests for material participation. You only need to satisfy one in a given tax year. We structure our clients around the most achievable test for their situation.

✦ Your Primary Test
101 hrs

Test 3: More than 100 hours, and not less than any other individual (including employees and contractors).

Alternative Test
500 hrs

Test 1: More than 500 hours of participation during the year. Most common for sole operators.

Alternative Test
Substantially All

Test 2: Substantially all participation in the activity is by you (and your spouse).

History-Based
5 of 10 yrs

Test 5: Materially participated in 5 of the prior 10 taxable years — useful for established investors.

⚠️ The "Not Less Than Anyone Else" Requirement — Test 3
For the 101-hour test to work, your hours must equal or exceed the hours of every other participant — including contractors, property managers, and on-site personnel. This is the detail most CPAs miss. It means you need to document your hours meticulously and understand how much time contractors are logging.

What Activities Count Toward the Hour Threshold

Not all time counts. The IRS looks for activities that are bona fide participation in the operations, management, or oversight of the solar activity. The key standard: would a non-owner in a similar capacity perform these same activities?

Activity Counts? Notes
✅ On-site inspection and oversight Yes Visiting the installation, reviewing performance, meeting contractors
✅ Contractor management Yes Reviewing bids, approving work, coordinating maintenance vendors
✅ Financial review and analysis Yes Reviewing energy production reports, invoices, P&L statements
✅ Regulatory and compliance review Yes Net metering agreements, utility interconnection, permit review
✅ Administrative management Yes Bookkeeping, insurance management, vendor correspondence
⚠️ Investor-level review Depends Reviewing quarterly summaries as a passive investor does NOT count — must be management activity
❌ Travel to and from the site No Commute time is excluded under Temp. Reg. §1.469-5T(f)(4)
❌ Investor activities at an investor's discretion No Activities not requiring owner input per Temp. Reg. §1.469-5T(f)(2)

IRS Documentation Standards

This is where most solar tax strategies fail an audit. The IRS requires contemporaneous records — meaning logs kept at or near the time the activity occurred, not reconstructed at year-end.

Temp. Reg. §1.469-5T(f)(4) — Proof of Participation
The extent of an individual's participation in an activity may be established by any reasonable means. Contemporaneous daily time reports, logs, or similar documents are not required if the extent of such participation may be established by other reasonable means. However, post-event "ballpark guesstimates" are not sufficient.

What Good Records Look Like

The IRS does not require a specific format, but audit-proof documentation typically includes:

  • Date of each activity
  • Nature of the activity — specific enough to explain what was done
  • Time spent — start and end, or total hours
  • Supporting evidence where available — emails, invoices, site photos, meeting notes
✅ Best Practice: Log each activity within 24–48 hours of occurrence. Courts have consistently upheld logs maintained in real time and rejected year-end reconstructions. Our client tracker below is designed specifically to meet this standard.

What to Avoid

  • Vague entries like "worked on solar project — 4 hours"
  • Round-number estimates (e.g., "approximately 2 hours every week")
  • Year-end summaries created before filing
  • No documentation at all — courts have denied deductions entirely

How We Structure Solar Investments for Compliance

Getting material participation right requires more than logging hours. The investment structure, entity type, and role of any property managers all affect whether losses are ultimately non-passive. Here's how we approach it:

Step 1 — Determine the right participation test

We assess your situation: do you have employees or contractors involved? How much time can you realistically commit? The 101-hour test (Test 3) is often the most achievable for investors with active management roles. The 500-hour test is better for single-owner operators with full control.

Step 2 — Structure contractor roles carefully

For Test 3, contractors' hours count against you. We help clients define contractor scopes narrowly — ensuring property managers handle only administrative tasks while you retain decision-making oversight. This protects your hour threshold without sacrificing operational efficiency.

Step 3 — Set up a contemporaneous logging system

We provide clients with our material participation tracker — a secure portal where you log activities in real time, categorize by type, and generate audit-ready reports. The system flags when you're on track and alerts you if you're falling behind the threshold.

Step 4 — Coordinate with bonus depreciation timing

IRC §168(k) bonus depreciation for solar equipment has a phase-down schedule. We coordinate the depreciation election with your material participation year to maximize the deduction in the year it generates the most tax benefit against your ordinary income.

What Happens in an Audit

IRS audits of passive activity claims are increasingly common as the IRS has identified solar tax strategies as an area of focus. Here's what to expect if you're selected:

  • The IRS will request your contemporaneous activity logs for the year in question
  • Agents will compare your claimed hours against any contractors' hours on Form 1099s or payroll records
  • They may interview contractors to verify the scope of their involvement
  • Without adequate documentation, deductions are disallowed and penalties of 20–40% may apply
⚠️ Accuracy penalty risk: If the IRS disallows a material participation claim for lack of documentation, the taxpayer typically owes a 20% accuracy-related penalty under IRC §6662 on top of the back taxes and interest. For a $500,000 deduction at 37%, that's $185,000 in taxes plus $37,000 in penalties.
